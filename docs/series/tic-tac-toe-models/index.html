The tic-tac-toe models

Over six weeks in the fall of 2026 I built the same small game four times, each time asking a harder question: can I train a model and publish it? Can I make it lean? Can a real neural network run on a Commodore 64? And can one be designed for the C64 from the start?

Tic-tac-toe is small enough to check every position, so every step could be tested exhaustively instead of sampled, and it's a game I had been teaching computers to play for more than a decade. All four names come from WarGames: Crystal Palace was the NORAD call sign where the computer plays against itself, and Dr. Stephen Falken built the AI.

Where it started

  • 2014: tic-tac-toe, an offline mobile game in JavaScript and HTML5 whose AI follows Newell and Simon's 1972 tic-tac-toe program, with Jasmine tests.
  • 2015: Tic-Tac-Toe in Second Life, with four levels from a random player to one that forks and blocks forks.
  • 2016: my first artificial neural network, written from scratch in JavaScript with a genetic algorithm (mutation and crossover) and XOR, AND and OR examples. Its tic-tac-toe trial pitted 200 small networks (9 inputs, 5 hidden, 9 outputs) against each other and against random and unbeatable bots, ranked by Elo; an illegal move ended the game as "Cheater!". For it I published two npm packages, octothorpe to draw the board and octothorpe-xo to play it, plus a rule-based tic-tac-toe AI.
  • 2019: 6502 assembly practice, including files for Ben Eater's 6502 breadboard computer, the chip the C64 work came back to.

What each step taught the next

Palace-9: learning to train and publish

I started training in the web browser, moved to custom CUDA and then PyTorch on my GPU, and learned to publish to Hugging Face and run on Ollama and llama.cpp. Fitting Ollama meant borrowing Qwen2-MoE's shape, and that format brought a 5,632-wide shared expert that made up most of the 648,528 parameters. The lesson: a standard runtime costs size I couldn't control.

Crystal-9: leaner, with its own runtime

Crystal-9 dropped the standard format for a 13-token vocabulary and a custom runtime: 24,726 parameters. I quantized it in stages to INT4 and INT3 with zero misses, and rejected any trial that missed even one move. Its 32-wide layers suited a GPU's tensor operations, but they were more than tic-tac-toe needed.

Crystal Palace 9: proving it runs on a C64

The first Crystal Palace 9 ran Crystal-9's original weights on a Commodore 64, loading them from disk during each move and computing in fixed-point 6502 assembly. It played complete games, and testing in real-ROM VICE caught bugs that a simpler emulator hid, such as an eight-bit memory offset that wrapped around. But all that disk loading made a computer-against-itself game take about 20 minutes.

Falken-9: designed for the C64 from the start

Falken-9 took every lesson at once: search the width, expert count and precision against what the C64 can afford; read the board instead of the move history; use symmetry to cut the problem to 627 boards; keep the whole model in memory; compute with exact integers. Then I probed the frozen network to see how it decides. Crystal Palace 9 now runs on it, and a full AI-vs-AI game takes just over a minute.
